Alice, feeling bored and sleepy while sitting with her sister, is startled when a talking White Rabbit with a pocket watch rushes past her. Driven by curiosity, she follows the rabbit down a deep hole, falling slowly through a space filled with shelves and maps. After landing safely, she pursues the rabbit into a long hall lined with locked doors, eventually discovering a tiny golden key on a glass table that fits a door leading to a beautiful garden. Too large to fit through the opening, Alice drinks from a bottle labeled 'DRINK ME,' which causes her to shrink, but she then finds she cannot reach the key on the table. She eats a cake marked 'EAT ME,' which causes her to grow to a massive size, leading her to weep large tears of frustration. When the White Rabbit returns, startled by her size, he drops his fan and gloves. Alice picks them up and discovers that using the fan makes her shrink again. Her shrinking eventually causes her to fall into a pool formed by her own tears, where she encounters a mouse swimming alongside her.

Alice begins by attempting to converse with a Mouse in a pool of tears, but inadvertently offends the creature by discussing her cat, Dinah, and a terrier she knows, both of which are natural predators to mice. After the Mouse swims away in annoyance, Alice leads a group of wet animals and birds—including a Dodo, a Duck, and a Lory—to the shore. To dry off, the Dodo proposes a 'Caucus-race,' a disorganized event where everyone runs in circles until they are dry. Alice is then declared the winner and distributes comfits as prizes, receiving her own thimble back as a prize. The Mouse attempts to tell a story, but Alice's misunderstandings lead to another argument, causing the Mouse to leave. Alice accidentally drives the remaining animals away by continuing to praise Dinah's skill at hunting birds and mice. Left alone, Alice encounters the White Rabbit, who mistakes her for his housemaid, 'Mary Ann,' and demands she retrieve his fan and gloves. Alice enters the Rabbit's house and, while searching for the items, drinks from a mysterious bottle that causes her to grow rapidly until she fills the entire room. Trapped inside, Alice overhears the White Rabbit attempting to enter the house. She manages to thwart him by grabbing at him through the window, causing him to fall. The Rabbit then calls for his gardener, Pat, to assist him in dealing with the giant intruder.

Trapped in the White Rabbit's house, Alice finds her arm filling the window. The Rabbit and his gardener, Pat, try to remove her, eventually deciding to send Bill the Lizard down the chimney. Alice kicks Bill, sending him flying, and the frustrated Rabbit threatens to burn the house down. Alice retaliates by throwing pebbles that transform into cakes. Eating one, she shrinks and escapes into a wood, where she encounters a giant puppy. After playing with it briefly, she wanders off and discovers a large mushroom topped by a hookah-smoking Caterpillar. The Caterpillar questions Alice’s identity and mocks her confusion regarding her changing size, eventually tasking her to recite 'You are old, Father William.' Offended by her incorrect recitation and annoyed by her desire to be taller than her current three-inch stature, the Caterpillar leaves, revealing that different sides of the mushroom will make her grow or shrink. Alice consumes pieces of the mushroom and experiences drastic growth, her neck becoming serpentine. This leads to an encounter with a paranoid Pigeon who insists that Alice is a serpent because she eats eggs. After convincing the bird of her humanity and struggling to manage her elongated neck, Alice uses the remaining mushroom pieces to restore herself to her original height.

After adjusting her size to nine inches, Alice approaches a small house and observes a conversation between a Fish-Footman and a Frog-Footman, who are delivering an invitation from the Queen to the Duchess. Alice enters the house to find a smoky, pepper-filled kitchen occupied by the Duchess, a baby, a cat, and a violent cook. The Duchess treats the baby harshly before abandoning it, leaving Alice to care for the infant. As Alice exits the house, the baby transforms into a pig and wanders off. Alice meets the Cheshire Cat, who explains that everyone in the area is mad, and learns that the cat intends to visit the Queen's croquet game. The cat vanishes slowly, leaving only its grin behind. Alice proceeds to the home of the March Hare, where she joins a bizarre and nonsensical tea party with the Hatter and a sleepy Dormouse. During the gathering, the Hatter asks Alice an unanswerable riddle, and they engage in frustrating circular arguments regarding the nature of their speech and the functionality of the Hatter’s broken watch.

The narrative begins at the Mad Hatter’s tea-party, where Alice engages in a confusing discussion about Time. The Hatter explains that he is stuck in eternal tea-time because he 'murdered' Time after a disagreement at the Queen of Hearts' concert. The Hatter and the March Hare force the sleepy Dormouse to tell a nonsensical story about three sisters who live in a treacle-well. Frustrated by the rudeness of the tea-party guests, Alice leaves and discovers a door in a tree that leads her back to the hall with the glass table. Using the golden key and the mushroom, she successfully enters the Queen's garden. There, she encounters three playing-card gardeners painting white roses red to avoid the Queen's wrath. When the royal procession arrives, the Queen discovers the error and orders the gardeners' execution; however, Alice hides them in a flower-pot, saving their lives. The Queen then invites Alice to a chaotic game of croquet, played with live hedgehogs, flamingoes, and soldiers doubling as arches. During the game, the Cheshire Cat’s head appears in the air to speak with Alice. She complains about the unfairness of the game and her dislike of the Queen, who is within earshot. The King eventually approaches the Cat, but finds its presence offensive, leading to a brief confrontation where Alice defends the Cat's right to exist.

In the Queen's garden, a dispute arises over the Cheshire Cat, whose disembodied head has appeared. The Queen orders it beheaded, the executioner argues he cannot behead a head without a body, and the King insists anything with a head can be executed. Alice suggests asking the Duchess, who is currently in prison. When the executioner returns with the Duchess, the Cat has vanished. Alice walks with the Duchess, who constantly attempts to find a moral in every situation. The Queen eventually interrupts, threatening the Duchess, who quickly disappears. Alice returns to the croquet game, where the Queen continues sentencing players to death until few remain. The Queen then introduces Alice to the Gryphon, who takes her to see the Mock Turtle. The Mock Turtle recounts his schooldays in the sea, describing subjects like 'Reeling and Writhing' and 'Uglification.' He and the Gryphon then explain the 'Lobster Quadrille,' a complex dance involving tossing lobsters into the sea. They demonstrate the first figure of the dance for Alice while the Mock Turtle sings a melancholic song about a whiting and a snail.

After the Mock Turtle and the Gryphon explain their nonsensical views on 'whiting'—claiming the fish hold their tails in their mouths because they were forced to dance, and that they provide the 'blacking' for shoes—they ask Alice to recount her adventures. Alice feels reluctant to revisit her past, as she has changed significantly since that morning, but she proceeds anyway. When asked to recite a poem, Alice finds her mind filled with the 'Lobster Quadrille,' causing the poem to come out as a nonsensical, altered version. The Mock Turtle and the Gryphon are baffled by her recital, and the Mock Turtle sings a melancholic song called 'Beautiful Soup.' The performance is interrupted by cries that a trial is beginning, and Alice is whisked away to the court of the King and Queen of Hearts. At the court, Alice observes the jury of birds and animals frantically taking notes. The Knave of Hearts is on trial for stealing tarts. The King serves as judge, and the White Rabbit acts as herald. The first witness, the Hatter, provides confusing and nervous testimony while Alice begins to grow in size. After the Hatter is dismissed and ordered to be beheaded, the Duchess’s cook is called as a witness. She claims the tarts are made of pepper, prompting the Dormouse to mention treacle, leading to chaos in the courtroom. Finally, the White Rabbit calls Alice herself as the next witness.

  Alice, having grown significantly larger, accidentally knocks over the jury-box while trying to testify, causing chaos in the courtroom. After she restores the jurymen, the King attempts to question her, but she claims to know nothing about the case. The White Rabbit then produces a mysterious, unsigned poem as evidence, which the King desperately tries to interpret as a confession of guilt from the Knave of Hearts. Alice dismisses the poem as meaningless nonsense. When the Queen demands the verdict before the sentence, Alice protests, leading the Queen to order her execution. Realizing that the entire court is merely a pack of cards, Alice confronts them and brushes them away, only to wake up on the riverbank with her sister. Alice recounts her strange dream, and her sister, left alone, reflects on the vivid nature of Alice's imagination and the enduring innocence of childhood, envisioning how Alice might one day share these same wondrous tales with future generations.
